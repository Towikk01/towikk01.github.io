How to Connect Your CRM to Telegram: 3 Practical Ways

The short version. There are three realistic ways to send CRM events to Telegram and receive replies back. 1) A native or marketplace integration: fastest, least flexible. 2) An automation platform such as n8n, Make or Zapier: no code, handles most cases. 3) A custom bot with webhooks: the most control, and the most upkeep. Start with the simplest one that covers your use case and move up only when it stops being enough.

Why connect a CRM to Telegram at all?

Telegram is where many teams and customers already spend the day, and a bot message is harder to miss than an email. Typical uses are internal alerts ("new lead from the pricing form"), reminders to a sales rep before a call, a status check from a phone ("what is open for Acme?"), and two-way chat with customers in markets where Telegram is the default messenger.

The CRM stays the source of truth. Telegram is the front door and the notification channel. Keep that split in mind and most design decisions get easier.

What do you need before you start?

  • A bot. Create it in Telegram with @BotFather and keep the token secret. Anyone with the token can control the bot.
  • A chat ID. The ID of the person, group or channel the bot writes to. A bot can message a user only after that user has started the chat with it, and a group bot must be added to the group first.
  • A trigger in the CRM. A workflow, automation rule or webhook that fires on the event you care about.
  • A rule for who may use it. Decide early whether the bot answers anyone or only known staff.

Way 1: How do native and marketplace integrations work?

Some CRMs ship a Telegram connector or list one in their app marketplace. You install it, paste the bot token, and pick which events post where. Check the marketplace of your CRM first; availability differs a lot between vendors.

Pros

  • Quickest route, often no maintenance on your side.
  • The vendor handles authentication and updates.

Cons

  • Usually limited to notifications. Replies and commands may not be supported.
  • You cannot change behavior the vendor did not plan for.
  • Not every CRM has one. HighLevel, for example, has no built-in Telegram channel according to its help documentation, so teams there need Way 2 or Way 3.

Example. A small agency installs a marketplace connector so every new deal posts "New deal: Acme, $4,000, owner Anna" into a team group. It works on day one, and nobody has to maintain it.

Way 2: How do you connect a CRM to Telegram with n8n, Make or Zapier?

Automation platforms sit between the CRM and Telegram. The CRM triggers a scenario, the platform formats a message, and a Telegram step sends it. The reverse also works: a Telegram trigger starts a flow that creates or updates a CRM record.

What the tools offer, based on their own documentation:

  • n8n has a Telegram node (send, edit, delete and pin messages, send files, answer callback queries) and a separate Telegram Trigger. You can self-host it.
  • Zapier lists a Telegram Bot app with a "New Message" trigger and actions to send a message, photo or poll, or react to a message. Sending supports up to five buttons.
  • Make also has a Telegram Bot app; check its current module list in your account.

A typical build

  1. Trigger: "deal moved to Won" in the CRM.
  2. Fetch the contact and owner, if the trigger does not carry them.
  3. Build the text, with the deal name, value and a link back to the CRM record.
  4. Send to the sales group chat.
  5. Add a log or error branch so a failed send does not vanish silently.

Pros

  • No server to run on hosted platforms. Visual editing that non-developers can follow.
  • Hundreds of other apps are one step away, so one scenario can also write to a sheet or a helpdesk.

Cons

  • Hosted tools bill by operations or tasks, so chatty flows get expensive.
  • Long scenarios become hard to debug.
  • Rate limits still apply (see the checklist below).

Way 3: When should you build a custom bot with webhooks?

Choose this when you need commands, menus, permissions or logic that platforms handle badly. The pattern: the CRM sends a webhook to your service, your service calls the Telegram Bot API, and for replies Telegram sends updates to your service, which calls the CRM API.

The Telegram side

  • Telegram can push updates to an HTTPS URL you register with setWebhook. Supported ports are 443, 80, 88 and 8443.
  • You can set a secret token. Telegram then sends it in the X-Telegram-Bot-Api-Secret-Token header of every request, so your service can reject anything else.
  • While a webhook is set, getUpdates (polling) does not work. Pick one mode.

The CRM side

Most CRMs can call a webhook from an automation, and they expose an API for writing back. Verify the API's rate limits before you design anything chatty. [internal link: Zoho CRM webhooks and API integrations]

Pros

  • Total control: commands, inline buttons, role checks, your own data model.
  • No per-task fees. Costs are hosting and developer time.

Cons

  • You own security, retries, logging and uptime.
  • Someone has to maintain it when the CRM or Telegram changes something.

Example. A manager types /deal Acme, the service looks the account up through the CRM API and replies with the stage, owner and next task, with a button to mark the task done. That interaction is awkward in a no-code flow and simple in code.

What should be on your checklist before going live?

  • Bot token stored as a secret, never in a public repo or a shared document.
  • Chat IDs tested for every destination (user, group, channel).
  • Message length kept short. A single Telegram text message is limited to 4096 characters, and the Bot FAQ advises staying under about one message per second in one chat and 20 per minute in a group.
  • Bulk sends throttled. Telegram's FAQ says bots cannot broadcast more than about 30 messages per second without paid broadcasts.
  • Personal data limited to what the recipient needs. Do not post full customer records into a group chat.
  • Webhook secret token checked, or the endpoint protected another way.
  • Retries and error alerts in place, so a failed delivery is noticed.
  • One owner named for the bot, and a plan for changing the token if it leaks.

Which way should you pick?

  • Need only alerts, and your CRM has a connector: Way 1.
  • Need alerts plus some automation, and a developer is not on hand: Way 2.
  • Need commands, permissions, customer-facing chat or high volume: Way 3.

Many teams start with Way 2 and replace only the parts that outgrow it. That is a sound path, because the CRM events and message texts you design for Way 2 carry over to a custom bot.

Frequently asked questions

Can a bot message a customer first?

No. A user has to start the conversation with the bot before it can write to them. Plan an opt-in step, such as a link on your site.

Is Telegram a good channel for customer support?

It depends on your audience. In markets where customers already use Telegram it works well; elsewhere email or WhatsApp may reach more people. Ask a sample of customers first.

Do I need a server?

Not for Way 1 or for hosted automation tools. For Way 3 you need a public HTTPS endpoint, which can be a small cloud function.

Is it safe to send CRM data to Telegram?

Treat Telegram messages as readable outside your CRM's permission model. Send short notifications with a link to the record, and keep sensitive fields out of the text.

Can I connect more than one CRM to the same bot?

Yes, but keep each source clearly labelled and use separate chats, or the team will lose track of where an alert came from.
